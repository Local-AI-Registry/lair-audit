## How to read this report

### The view from above, and how it's written

Most research on AI search reports one study at a time. This report does the opposite. It gathers every bucket of evidence we could find on how local businesses get recommended by AI, lays them on one table, and steps back until the whole picture resolves. From that altitude, the disconnected facts collapse into a single conclusion and four currents that run beneath all of them.

Because the goal is to be useful to a business owner and defensible to a skeptic, every section is written twice. In Plain Terms states the point in language anyone can act on. The Proof then establishes it rigorously, naming the specific researcher, study, court record, or dataset behind each claim, with the chart that shows it.

### How every claim is labelled

- **CITED** A figure from a named, linkable source. Real or omitted, never invented.
- **ANALYSIS** Our own conclusion from reading the sources together.
- **PROJECTION** A forward inference from a measured trend. Flagged, never disguised as data.

## I · The Convergence, eleven streams, one substrate

### In Plain Terms

We pulled together every kind of evidence we could find about how AI decides which local businesses to recommend, what shoppers do, what the data shows, what Google's own people and documents say, what scientists have proven about how these systems retrieve information, and what the law now requires. They come from totally different places and were never meant to be read together. But when you put them side by side, they all say the same thing: AI doesn't recommend a website, a profile, or whoever is closest. It recommends a business it can recognize as real, one that many trustworthy sources independently describe the same way. Being that recognizable, verifiable business is the whole game.

## II · Current One, the battlefield left your property

**It's no longer about what you publish**

### In Plain Terms

For twenty years, "doing your SEO" meant working on things you owned: your website, your pages, your Google profile. That era is ending. The things that now make AI recommend you mostly live off your own property, what other people say about you on YouTube, in reviews, in community threads, in local round-ups. You've gone from building your storefront to earning a reputation you don't fully control. The job is no longer to publish more about yourself; it's to be talked about, consistently, by others.

### The Proof

Glenn Allsopp's Ahrefs analysis of 75,000 brands ranked every candidate signal by how strongly it correlates with AI visibility. The signals describing what others say about you, YouTube mentions (0.737), branded web mentions (0.66-0.71), sit at the top. The signals describing what you publish about yourself, your domain rating (0.266), the backlinks you build (0.218), the pages you publish (0.194), sit at the bottom.² The gap is not subtle.

0.66-0.71branded web mentions. CITED: Glenn Allsopp, Ahrefs, AI visibility study, 75,000 brands.
0.266your domain rating. CITED: Glenn Allsopp, Ahrefs, AI visibility study, 75,000 brands.
0.218the backlinks you build. CITED: Glenn Allsopp, Ahrefs, AI visibility study, 75,000 brands.
0.194the pages you publish. CITED: Glenn Allsopp, Ahrefs, AI visibility study, 75,000 brands.

**Figure II · Off-property signals win.** Ahrefs, 75,000 brands (Glenn Allsopp), 2026.

The mechanism, disclosed in Judge Amit Mehta's November 2025 ruling in U.S. v. Google, explains why. Google grounds its Gemini answers through a retriever called FastSearch, built on a deep-learning model, RankEmbed, that matches queries to documents semantically rather than by counting links.¹⁷ Google's own VP of Search, Robby Stein, describes the front end as "query fan-out", the model issues dozens of background searches and synthesizes what it retrieves.¹² Neither process rewards a business for publishing more pages about itself; both reward a business that the wider web independently corroborates. **ANALYSIS** The practical relocation is total: the battlefield moved from your domain to the ecosystem's consensus about you.

## III · Current Two, specificity is rewarded at every layer

**Generic loses; specific wins, everywhere**

### In Plain Terms

The single most repeated pattern across all the evidence is this: the more specific you are, the more AI rewards you, and it shows up at every level. Specific questions get AI answers more often than vague searches. First-hand, specific content beats generic listicles. A review that names exactly what you did ("great with anxious dogs") is worth more than a bare five stars. And a business known for one clear thing beats one that claims to do everything. Being known for the right thing isn't a tagline, it's the rule the whole system runs on.

### The Proof

The same generic-to-specific gradient appears independently at four layers. At the query layer, Ahrefs' study of 146 million SERPs shows single-word searches trigger an AI Overview 9.5% of the time, while full question-style queries trigger 57.9%.³ At the content layer, Google's own Search Central guidance explicitly rewards "non-commodity content" and first-hand experience over generic aggregation.¹⁰ At the review layer, AI answers quote the specific language of reviews verbatim, the named service, not the star count. At the entity layer, the retrieval-science consensus (LUKE-style entity grounding) favors precisely-described, unambiguous entities.³⁰

**Figure III · The specificity ladder.** Query: Ahrefs. Content: Google. Review/entity: platform mechanics & LAIR analysis.

This is the academic and mechanical basis for Local AI Registry's founding principle, alignment, not coverage. **ANALYSIS** A business fails in AI not by being invisible but by being known for the wrong, generic thing. The corrective is specificity, applied deliberately at all four layers at once.

## IV · Current Three, four clocks, one transition window

**Four unrelated timers strike at once**

### In Plain Terms

Four completely separate things are all changing at the same moment, roughly 2026 into 2027. How many people use AI for local search. How open AI is to recommending smaller businesses. How people phrase their searches. And when the new AI transparency laws kick in. None of these has anything to do with the others, which is exactly why their lining up matters. When four unrelated clocks all strike the same hour, that's not coincidence; it's a transition. The businesses that move during the window get an advantage the latecomers won't.

### The Proof

Four independently-measured trends converge on the same window. Adoption: BrightLocal records consumer use of AI for local discovery rising from 6% to 45% across 2025-26.¹ The citation window: Ahrefs finds the share of AI-Overview citations drawn from the organic top ten fell from ~76% to ~38%, widening the pool to the long tail, a window the retrieval-bias literature predicts will re-narrow.⁴,²⁵ The exposure runway: as phrasing turns conversational, local AI-trigger rates climb off their 7.9% floor toward the 57.9% question rate.³ Regulation: the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency duties take effect 2 August 2026.³⁸

## V · Current Four, the accountability vacuum

**The Keystone · No one can tell you what the AI says**

### In Plain Terms

Here's the strangest part. Everything above says your AI reputation now matters enormously, but nobody can actually tell you what it is. Ask the same question twice and the AI gives different answers. The platforms don't report which businesses they recommend or why. There's no record that follows you across ChatGPT, Gemini, and Google. And the law polices AI that lies to customers, not AI that's wrong about you. So the one thing that now decides whether customers find you is the one thing no one is keeping score of. That gap is the whole reason a registry needs to exist.

### The Proof

Four properties you would expect of any system of record are absent across every major AI surface. Reproducibility: practitioners document that AI Overview results "aren't repeatable" run-to-run, and Google's own Search Console does not separate AI-driven clicks. Accuracy: Nelson Liu and Percy Liang (Stanford) found only 51.5% of generated sentences are fully supported by their citations, and the Tow Center at Columbia found leading AI search tools wrong on more than 60% of queries.³²,³⁴ Cross-platform record: none exists. Regulatory scope: the FTC and EU AI Act target deception of consumers, not accuracy about third-party businesses.³⁵,³⁸

51.5%of generated sentences are fully supported by their citations. CITED: N. F. Liu, T. Zhang, P. Liang (Stanford), Evaluating Verifiability in Generative Search Engines.
More than 60%of queries: leading AI search tools wrong. CITED: Tow Center at Columbia, AI Search Has a Citation Problem.

**Figure V · The accountability vacuum.** LAIR analysis of platform reporting, reproducibility & regulatory scope, 2026.

This is the keystone the other three currents rest on. **ANALYSIS** The entire body of evidence converges on "be a verifiable entity," moving fast, on a closing clock, while no neutral party measures whether you are one, reproducibly, across platforms, over time. That negative space, visible only when all eleven streams are held in one frame, is not a gap in the research. It is a description of an institution that does not yet exist.
