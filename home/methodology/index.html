Methodology: How the AI Score Is Built

By Steve Lee, founder of Local AI Registry · Updated November 15, 2026

The thesis

One number. One mechanism.

AI assistants don’t recommend the closest business, the prettiest website, or whoever paid the most. They recommend a business they can retrieve and recognize as real: one that many independent, trustworthy sources describe the same way.

Because retrieval is the gate everything else passes through, the single heaviest signal is whether you appear in the results an AI model pulls from when it answers. Everything else either earns that retrievability or refines what the AI says once you’re in.

Retrievability isn’t one ranking factor among many. It’s the precondition for all of them.

The weights

How much each signal weighs.

Eight signals, identical for every business, ordered by how much each one moves an AI recommendation. The single heaviest is whether you can be retrieved at all.

Retrievability and the attestation that earns it are the retrieval tier, together 46% of the score. Nearly half.

It measures how likely AI engines are to recommend a business, and how accurately. The same method scores every business on the registry. Every business is scored the same way, and nobody can pay for a better score. Fair starts at 45, good at 65.

Reading a business page

What the marks on a business page mean

Every fact on a business page carries one of three marks, and every mark points to a reference at the end of that page.

Confirmedsolid circle
From a known source: the business's website, its Google Business Profile, review sites like Yelp, Healthgrades and RealSelf, a licensing board, or measured by Local AI Registry.
Visitor submittedhollow circle
Added or corrected by a person, with the date and how they know, like a phone call or a visit.
AI guesshollow triangle
What AI engines like ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity are saying right now. Nobody has confirmed it.

Each row is marked Confirmed, Visitor submitted or AI guess, with where it came from. Each reference names the fact, the source, what kind of source it is, and the date it was checked, with a link and an archived copy so anyone can verify it. Visitor references are dated firsthand accounts. AI guesses record what an AI engine said on that date, not what is true.

How AI reads a business

How AI decides what to tell you about a med spa

When you ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Google for a med spa, the AI doesn't visit each business. It reads what's already written about it: the business's website, its Google Business Profile, review sites like Yelp and RealSelf, news stories, licensing boards and directories like ours. Then it repeats what those sources agree on.

That's why AI gets small facts wrong. If Yelp says a place is open Mondays and the website says it isn't, the AI has to pick one. Each business page on the registry lists the answers that disagree with what we've confirmed.

The more sources agree, and the more specific they are, the more confidently AI recommends a business. A review that says "the nurse did half a syringe in my lips and it looks natural" is far more useful to an AI than one that says "great place."

How AI decides what to tell you about an eye surgery practice

When you ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Google for an eye surgeon, the AI doesn't visit the practice. It reads what's already written about it: the practice's website, its Google Business Profile, review sites like Healthgrades and Yelp, your state's medical and optometry boards, news stories and directories like ours. Then it repeats what those sources agree on.

That's why AI gets small facts wrong. If one site says a surgeon still operates there and the practice's own website doesn't list them, the AI has to pick one. Each business page on the registry lists the answers that disagree with what we've confirmed.

The more sources agree, and the more specific they are, the more confidently AI recommends a practice. A review that says "my surgeon did my cataract surgery on a Tuesday and I was driving by Friday" is far more useful to an AI than one that says "great doctor."

How AI decides what to tell you about a local business

When you ask ChatGPT, Gemini or Google about a local business, the AI doesn't visit it. It reads what's already written about it: the business's website, its Google Business Profile, review sites, news stories and directories like ours. Then it repeats what those sources agree on.

That's why AI gets small facts wrong. If Yelp says a place is open Mondays and the website says it isn't, the AI has to pick one. Each business page on the registry lists the answers that disagree with what we've confirmed.

The more sources agree, and the more specific they are, the more confidently AI recommends a business. A review that names who helped and what they did tells an AI far more than one that says "great place."

Before you choose

How to choose a med spa, with or without AI

A few checks take ten minutes and matter more than any rating. This is general information, not medical advice.

  1. 01Look up every injector. Doctors are listed by your state's medical board, and nurses and nurse practitioners by its nursing board. Both lookups are free.
  2. 02Ask who does the injections, and check that person's license.
  3. 03Ask for the price per unit of Botox and per syringe of filler, not per area.
  4. 04Read reviews that name a provider and a treatment. Vague praise is easy to fake.
  5. 05Check that the hours and phone number match on Google and the website. Mismatches are how people end up at a locked door.
  6. 06Ask whether they dissolve filler and when they would tell you not to add more.

How to choose an eye surgery practice, with or without AI

A few checks take ten minutes and matter more than any rating. This is general information, not medical advice.

  1. 01Look up the surgeon who'll operate on you. Your state's medical board lists every ophthalmologist, and its optometry board lists the optometrists who do your exams and follow-ups. Both lookups are free.
  2. 02Ask who does the surgery itself and who sees you before and after it. At many practices those are different people.
  3. 03Ask which laser or lens they use for your procedure, and why it suits your eyes.
  4. 04Ask what the consult covers, how long it takes, and whether your eyes will be dilated so you need a ride home.
  5. 05Ask what your insurance pays for and what it leaves you to pay. Cataract surgery is often covered and LASIK usually is not.
  6. 06Read reviews that name a surgeon and a procedure. Vague praise is easy to fake.

How to choose a local business, with or without AI

A few checks take ten minutes and tell you more than any rating.

  1. 01Check that the hours and phone number match on Google and the business's own website. Mismatches are how people end up at a locked door.
  2. 02Read reviews that name a person and what they did. Vague praise is easy to fake.
  3. 03Look for recent reviews and recent posts. A page nobody has touched in a year may not match the business today.
  4. 04Ask for the price before you go, and ask what it includes.
  5. 05Where the work needs a license, ask whose license it is and look it up with your state.
